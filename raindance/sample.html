CHAPTER THREE

A Man Apart

Ali woke from his alcohol-induced slumber and immediately reached for the bottle of whisky next to his bed. He sat up, trading the cold tile floor for his wooden bed frame. Waking always brought the same sequence of emotions: disappointment, apprehension, then flight. The bottle delivered two heavy gulps on command.

Grandpa accused him of drinking like a man running from something.

Something.

The thought almost made him laugh. He wasn’t running from something, he was running from many things. Armies of memory waited at the edge of his consciousness. Intoxication stood guard, and he couldn’t lower it, not even for a job interview. Grandpa complained constantly, but he wasn’t the one facing the bloody army.

Another gulp.

He reached for the cigarette pack on the three-legged stool, a survivor somehow functioning despite its missing limb. Ali wasn’t one to fix broken things; he had more important battles. He slid out a cigarette and clicked the lighter, then caught sight of the time on his phone’s display. Grandpa would return soon. No time for another lecture. He pocketed the cigarette and took another gulp instead.

His superhuman metabolism frustrated him most here. It took excessive amounts of alcohol to achieve any effect. An expensive habit for a man who was broke with a capital B.

His stomach sent urgent signals to his brain. He rose and headed downstairs. At the bottom of the staircase, he found Grandpa in the parlour with a woman.

Through his haze, Ali’s first thought was: prostitute. But that didn’t sit right; she wore a professional skirt-suit. His muddled mind cycled through possibilities before settling on the obvious: a visitor. Even drunk, he could sense no intimacy in his grandfather’s demeanor.

None of his business. He offered a greeting and continued toward the kitchen.

“Gabriel.”

His formal name. Serious business, then.

Damn. Why now? What complaint this time? He turned, eyes fixed on the faded red carpet with its patches of brown threadwork where wear had won.

“She needs your help,” Grandpa said, holding up Ali’s old card.

Ali shot Grandpa a look that said: kitchen, now.

“Why would you bring her here?” Ali demanded as soon as they entered the fluorescent-lit kitchen.

“She needs your help.”

“You know I’m not interested.”

“Please, Gabriel. Just hear her out.”

“I don’t need to hear her out. Whatever she wants, whoever she needs found—husband, brother, lover—I’m not interested.” The words tumbled out, defensive and final. Grandpa had been there. He’d seen everything. Why did this need explaining?

Grandpa’s eyes held on him a moment too long, something unreadable moving behind them. Then he laid a hand on Ali’s shoulder and left without another word.

In a more sober state, Ali might have dwelt on the intrusion. But hunger drove all else from his mind. The loaf of bread on the counter offered possibilities: egg sandwiches, maybe noodles…

Who was he kidding? He grabbed the bread, retrieved peanut butter from the fridge, and left.

Artificial lavender hung thick in the parlour air, a poor mask to shield evidence of Ali’s smoking. Grandpa still sat with the woman, apparently consoling her. None of Ali’s business. He kept walking.

“Please don’t desert me…”

Something in her voice made him pause.

“My husband has been missing for six days. You’re my only hope. No one else will help—”

That something grew inside him as she approached.

“—my children need their father.”

He turned. Her mascara-stained face and pleading eyes made that nameless feeling, that same something inside him, swell. Despite the alcohol meant to shield him from the pain of others, her desperation was penetrating his defenses.

“I won’t lie to you,” Ali said. “I can’t help. Nothing you say will change that.”

Confusion flickered across her features. She’d clearly read something different in his expression than what his words conveyed.

“I know that sounds cruel, but I don’t mean it to be.” He backed away as he spoke, needing distance from what he felt radiating from her.

“To find your husband, I’d have to open myself to pain. Not just yours, but hundreds of others. I really can’t explain the science. It’s something I used to do, but now… I carry enough pain for a lifetime. I just can’t. I’m sorry.”

Her face fell, hope visibly draining away. Ali couldn’t bear to watch another person’s suffering up close. Not when it threatened to break through his carefully constructed walls.

“I’m truly sorry,” he said once more, then fled upstairs.

* * *

Okoro watched the woman gather her things, shoulders slumped in defeat. Her hands trembled as she lifted her bag.

Okoro felt torn. He understood Ali’s position; he’d witnessed the tragedies that broke him. But his heart ached for this woman heading toward the door. Shame washed over him for initially seeing her as merely a story rather than a person in crisis.

As if reading his thoughts, she turned back one final time. “Please help me.”

Those three words, barely whispered, caused Okoro to stagger. He ran his fingers through his Afro, knowing he couldn’t change Ali’s mind.

“I wish I could do more. As I said, he’s lost. A year ago, he was different, but now…” He paused. An idea formed.

“The only way I can help is by forcing the authorities to act. Media pressure sometimes works. If the story reaches the front page… You mentioned his case involves a politician. Honourable Attah, right?”

He pulled out his notepad, no longer hunting news but trying to save a life.

The woman’s eyes widened slightly, the first hint of hope he’d seen in them since she’d entered his home.

CHAPTER THIRTEEN

The Rainmaker

At 2 PM on October 4th, 2022, Abuja was introduced to the Rainmaker, and with him, the revolution no one saw coming.

The Horizon TV logo dissolved into something unprecedented: a massive mural of Fela Kuti dominated the screen, the revolutionary musician’s iconic image painted in bold strokes across an entire wall. Across his chest, three lines of text burned with prophetic fire:

Set the country on fire
Let it burn
From the ashes will rise a new one

The camera pulled back to reveal the full scope of the mural, then a figure emerged from the shadows. Dressed entirely in black—boots, cargo pants, tactical vest—he moved with military precision to the center of the frame. His face bore a crying white mask with a mournful expression, twin tears of red carved down its cheeks. Two holes revealed eyes that burned with absolute conviction. A third opening allowed his voice to carry the weight of history.

“Citizens of the hopeless Republic of Nigeria.” His tone was dripping with accumulated rage. “For far too long, you have accepted suffering as your birthright. The time has come for you to say ‘No more!’”

He stepped closer to the camera.

“I’m here today to show you just how powerless your so-called leaders truly are. To liberate you from the illusion of bondage you have long lived in, an illusion fueled and perpetuated by these very leaders who owe you accountability.”

“For decades you have been robbed of your birthright—your oil, your land, your dignity. First by colonizers, then by your own countrymen who learned their lessons too well. While innocents die for lack of basic healthcare, these thieves build mansions. While students learn in crumbling schools, they send their children overseas. They have built empires on the graves of your children.”

He gestured to the mural behind him.

“The great Fela tried to wake you with his music. Today, I bring you fire.”

The screen cut to black. When the image returned, it showed the president’s official portrait filling the frame. Without warning, flames consumed the photograph from the edges inward until nothing remained but ash.

From those ashes, a new image emerged: a concrete room filled with prisoners in chains.

The camera panned across terrified faces; faces the elite knew intimately—their sons, daughters, spouses, relatives. Among them sat Farida Abubakar, the Senate President’s daughter.

At this point, a warning rolled across the screen: WARNING! GRAPHIC CONTENT!

Four prisoners were dragged to a raised platform in the center of the room. Their pleas echoed in the concrete space as masked guards forced them to their knees. Four executioners entered the frame, rifles raised. The camera zoomed in on the backs of their black shirts, each bearing a single white word:

mismanagement
of
security
funds

What happened next drew a national gasp. ‘Mismanagement of Security Funds’ gunned down the four prisoners. As the bodies fell, the Rainmaker’s voice cut through the horrified silence:

“This is justice for every soldier sent to fight terrorists you secretly fund, for every officer who died protecting a system that betrayed them, for the children who perished in hospitals and schools while you fed chaos with stolen security funds. And this…” The camera found his masked face one final time. “This is just the beginning.”

It was a masterwork of psychological warfare executed with impeccable showmanship, and it generated its intended effect.

Across the nation, social media erupted. #Rainmaker began trending as social feeds filled with shaky phone recordings of TV screens. Video apps exploded with reaction clips, group chats buzzed with frantic conversations, and timelines filled with people trying to make sense of what they’d just watched. The entire country watched and discussed history unfolding in real time.

The Rainmaker had weaponized the very technology they’d used to pacify the masses. By withholding the captives’ names, he ensured his message would spread like wildfire through shares, retweets, and desperate searches for answers.

Nigeria would remember this day as the moment everything changed, the day a masked man brought revolution to their screens, and fear to those who thought themselves untouchable.

This is just the beginning.
