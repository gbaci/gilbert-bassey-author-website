Prologue

Two Meetings

Two meetings that day would change Michael’s life forever, though he wouldn’t know it for a year.

Amaka knew what was coming. How could she not? The walls had ears, and she had long mastered the art of listening. But still, her heart leapt when the door to Michael’s office flung open. It sank when Boyé stormed out, his face twisted with a rage she’d never seen in their three years as colleagues.

On instinct, she rose to intercept him and offer comfort, because what else could she do? He’d just been fired for trying to help a woman in need, and the least she could do was help numb the pain, however little.

“He is a devil,” Boyé announced, walking past Amaka’s outstretched arms.

Against her better judgment, she’d harbored faint hope that her impassioned speech might sway Michael’s mind. But he had proven once again to be a man who did exactly as he pleased, others be damned. A powerful quality, certainly, but like all power, dark shadows followed in its wake.

On a different day, she would have felt compelled to go after Boyé, to make sure he was alright. But this was today, and the woman in black was still inside with Michael. She would need help soon.

⁂

“Please, I need this money. My baby is dying,” the woman in black begged, her voice raw against the wooden walls of the immaculate office.

Dressed in an expertly-tailored black suit, no tie, Michael replied, his deep baritone carrying an honesty that was as heartfelt as it was devoid of compassion, “I’m sorry, madam, but I can’t…”

“I have to save him,” she broke in.

Michael pressed on, “help you. Policy demands we investigate your husband’s death.”

“Oga, don’t tell me about company policy!” she half-shouted, slamming her hand on the desk.

A tense silence fell. The AC hummed in the corner, cooling the thick air. Having realized what she’d just done, the woman regained some of her composure. Well, as much as she could under the circumstances.

“I’m begging for my baby,” she whispered, tears falling.

“Nothing I can do,” Michael said, voice flat, unmoved by her tears. He frowned, annoyed, wondering where Amaka was. She should have been listening.

The woman dropped to her knees. “You can do something,” she sobbed. “Mr. Boyé promised me the money today, but you fired him! Please don’t do this.”

Amaka stepped in just then. Seeing the woman sobbing on the floor, she glared at Michael as she rushed to the woman’s side.

“Help me,” the woman said, “please beg him to save my baby.”

Experience had taught Amaka that different people required different consolation methods, and she had also learned to identify which approach suited whom. She opted to offer quiet consolation. She bent down and pulled the woman up while gently patting her shoulders exactly as her mother would have done.

She held the woman in front of her, meeting her tear-filled gaze with a look that said: I’m really sorry. If I could help you, I would. She led the woman in black out of the office, shooting Michael one last scowl as she crossed the doorway.

Michael couldn’t care less; he was leaving for lunch.

⁂

The upscale restaurant hummed with the quiet chatter of late-lunch diners. Michael sat in the corner, half-listening to his phone call, waiting for his meal. Soft pop ballads from the 2000s drifted from hidden speakers; Leona Lewis was Bleeding Love.

“You may think I’m cruel, but decency doesn’t pay the bills,” he said into his phone as a petite waitress in well-fitted black pants and white shirt, punctuated with ankara at the seams, approached with a black wooden tray.

“Our loyalty—call it kindness if you like—belongs to the company, not the client.” He watched the waitress with absent interest.

“Why?” He gave a light laugh, mocking the other’s ignorance. “Because clients don’t fund the expensive vacations or luxury cars.”

“Excuse me,” Michael called to the departing waitress.

“I’ll call you back.”

“Yes sir,” she returned with her practiced smile.

“I ordered chicken fried rice.” He gestured at the food. A small vase of red hibiscus sat beside the plates.

The waitress checked her tablet, her smile unwavering.

“I’m sorry, sir, but you ordered Chinese fried rice, peppered chicken, and fruit salad,” she said, her voice small but warm.

“So I’m lying?” He looked up at her, his tone remained calm.

“No sir, that’s not… I’m sorry.” Her face flushed with anxiety.

“I’d like to speak to the manager.”

Goosebumps pricked her neck.

“Please, sir, I’ll get you the Chicken fried rice. If he thinks I messed up, I’ll lose my job.”

Michael stood, ignoring her plea.

“Stop acting like a brat and sit down,” a voice cut in.

He froze mid-stride, stunned by such directness. He never tolerated embarrassment. Turning to locate this audacious speaker, anger surged through him, venomous retorts forming.

What he saw extinguished his fury instantly. Before him sat a woman whose beauty he’d never encountered before. Her slender frame and luminous black skin seemed to absorb the restaurant’s light, radiating it back with ethereal glow. His legs carried him toward her table before his mind registered the movement.

“Nice to meet you too. What do you say we turn this into a date?” he said, deploying his practiced charm. This’ll be easy, he thought.

“I only date decent men,” she replied without looking up from her medical journal, ignoring him with perfected indifference.

“And what will it take to make me decent?” he countered with a practiced smile.

“More than you can afford.”

“I can afford quite a lot.”

Then she raised her eyes—eyes that seemed to hold magic within them. She held his gaze briefly before directing it toward the distressed waitress. “Can you?”

He caught her meaning and conceded, signaling the waitress to bring his food. Then he pulled out a chair, making himself her uninvited companion.

She gave him a questioning look. “Courtesy requires permission before you join another’s table.”

“Courtesy demands many unnecessary things from society.”

“Your opinion.”

“My fact,” he replied, smiling more than he intended. She maintained her composed expression, but something in her eyes suggested she might be more amused than she let on.

“Any other facts to share?” she asked with feigned interest.

“My name is Michael Azodo, and you will be my wife.”

She laughed then, not a polite chuckle, but a genuine laugh that transformed her face and, oddly enough, made him want to hear it again.

Chapter One

Blood On His Hands

Out of the black void sprang a blinding white light that dissolved into the yellow glare of the sun shining on a familiar red-brick building. The deceptive tranquility, broken only by chirping birds and the breeze rustling through the tall green trees, carried an ominous weight, as if the scene itself were holding its breath.

With no forewarning, it came, as it always did: a piercing scream, terrifying in its rawness. It filled his world, bringing that familiar dread that never faded until this phase of the nightmare had run its course.

Even though he’d experienced it over a hundred times now, the dread still paralysed him, making it hard to breathe. It gave him the feeling that something terrible had happened, or was going to happen.

Then came the fall.

From the top floor of the ten-story red-brick building, looking down from the ledge, a boy fell. The image wavered—sometimes sharp, sometimes hazy—like a painting struggling to find its focus. The closer the falling boy got to the ground, the louder the scream became until it was unbearable. Just when he thought his ears would give out, the black void offered escape, enveloping him in its emptiness. But only for a while, because then came the guilt.

Sometimes the fall came first, other times last. Either way, one always accompanied the other; inseparable twins walking hand in hand to deliver their gifts of fear and guilt, shame and pain.

The guilt was the worst part. While the terror made it hard to breathe, the guilt hollowed him out completely, leaving him wishing he’d never existed at all. This feeling tore at his mind long before the accompanying image emerged from the void.

The desolate office space was choked with file cabinets overflowing with papers and worn manila folders. The heavy air hung with dankness, lending everything an unearthly quality. There a boy sat in the corner, knees pressed tight against his chest, muttering in rhythmic repetition, “it was a mistake...it was a mistake...it was a mistake...” But this time, something was different. There was blood on his hands.

And with that revelation, he came into the light.

⁂

Michael jerked awake in the green-tinted darkness, his heart pounding. His Iron Man nightshirt clung to him, soaked in cold sweat. The split-unit’s indicator glowed green, confirming the air conditioner was working despite the heat he felt. After a few deep breaths to orient himself, he turned on the bedside lamp and pulled open the first of three drawers. He rummaged inside before extracting a white prescription bottle with cursive handwriting on the label:

For your dreams

It was empty. Shit!

He’d woken up in the middle of the night enough times to know that trying to go back to sleep was pointless. His body simply wouldn’t allow it. He had two choices: finish the movie he’d been watching before bed or play video games until dawn. Opting to decide in the parlor, he turned off the light and left the room, but not before taking a moment to look at his sleeping wife. Even in darkness, Rebecca was beautiful.

Chapter Two

Happily Ever After

Rebecca stood in her kitchen, feeling weirdly happy as morning sunlight streamed through the window above the grey sink. Four months into her marriage to Michael Azodo, she found herself consistently surprised by her contentment. Before the wedding—that spectacular affair at Maitama’s domed cathedral—she had anticipated frequent conflict, not this easy companionship that had developed between them.

She broke eggs into the transparent ceramic mixing bowl Amaka had given them as a wedding gift, her hands moving with the practiced efficiency that had earned her “Kitchen Ninja”, Michael’s nickname for her. Pancakes, eggs, and oatmeal for now; yam flour disguised as pounded yam and her mother’s spectacular egusi recipe for later. The recipes flowed through her fingers without conscious thought, techniques her mother had taught her long before.

A sudden clatter behind her made her jump. The last egg slipped from her fingers, shattering on the floor. Rebecca turned toward the sound with a flash of annoyance.

“You should see your face right now.” Michael leaned against the doorway, his expression alight with amusement.

“You owe me one egg,” she said, half-sulking.

“I’ll gladly trade one crate for a point,” he said with a victorious smile, walking to what he’d christened their “Wall of Moments.”

Rebecca watched as he updated their game scorecard on the refrigerator; a competition they’d started two months before the wedding. Seven months ago, the double-door smart-fridge had been just a large grey appliance. Michael had transformed it into their visual history, documenting what he considered their best moments together. His two strict rules: only pictures of them together were allowed, and each addition had to be distinctly different from all others.

The collection had grown steadily: the photo from their second date—the only conventionally romantic image of the lot; another from their wedding day showing Michael’s laughing face peeking from beneath her Cinderella-inspired gown; several from hiking adventures; one from their Dubai trip undertaken because Michael had specific burger cravings.

Michael updated their unnamed game’s scorecard on the fridge’s edge:

Month 6, Week 3

Michael [2 - 4] Rebecca

They still couldn’t agree on a name. Michael had suggested “Game of Love” which was too cheesy for her taste. She’d countered with “Couple Kombat,” but he’d rejected it immediately, saying it conjured images of blood. Rebecca almost smiled at the irony.

“You’re still going to lose,” she said, pouring oil into the pan. “Why bother?”

“Planning the greatest comeback in comeback history.” He turned toward the door.

“Oshey! Comeback Master!” she teased. “Come back and help me cook.”

Rebecca watched his face as he tried to maintain his composure. She knew exactly how to break through his facade. Her particular brand of silliness never failed to win a smile from him, if not a full laugh. He returned to her side, where she put him to work flipping pancakes in the sizzling pan.

“Did the dreams drive you from the room again?” she asked, softening her tone. His nightmares troubled her, though not for reasons he would ever suspect.

Michael nodded.

“What about the Queen’s elixir?”

“It’s finished. I plan to get a refill at the party.” He glanced at her. “Speaking of, what time should I pick you up?”

“What party?” she asked blankly, before his bewildered look jogged her memory. The birthday. His mother. “Oh! Do I have to come?”

Rebecca turned back to the sink quickly, rinsing the mixing bowl to hide her expression. The thought of spending an evening with Lydia made her stomach tighten. Ever since she’d met Michael’s mother, she’d avoided the woman whenever possible. There was something knowing in Lydia’s eyes, something that made Rebecca feel exposed.

“It’s her birthday,” Michael said simply.

“That’s a good point,” Rebecca conceded with a sigh. Of all the performances her role required, the dutiful daughter-in-law was the most challenging.

⁂

Later that morning they sat at their dining table, an imposing glass-top supported by four ornately carved black wooden legs, nestled in a spacious alcove within the living room wall. Michael was dressed for work in his signature black suit and no tie, while Rebecca remained in her white nightgown, her black braided hair with blue streaks contained by a simple hair net.

Her mind drifted to his nightmare as she cut a small slice of pancake.

“You know? Your dream could be a prediction.”

Michael raised an eyebrow, curious.

“It’s probably forecasting that I’ll be a widow soon,” she continued casually, watching him. “With the blood on your hands and all.”

She saw him relax as he recognized her morbid humor. His laugh bounced off the beige concrete walls.

“From nightmare to widowhood? What an imagination! And Amaka thinks I’m the mean one.”

Rebecca shrugged. “I’ll hold the record for fastest widow.”

This cracked him up. “Is there even an award for that? In any case, I know a woman whose husband died at their wedding reception.”

“Liar!” she challenged, though she knew he wouldn’t lie to her. He had made that promise, and oddly enough, he’d kept it. It was one of the few aspects of Michael that had surprised her. He remained consistent even in small matters of principle.

“Honest to God. And the weirdest part is… She killed him.”

“You’re lying,” Rebecca countered instinctively. “And even if it were true, she’s disqualified.”

“Believe what you want,” Michael said, “but you’re lucky to have such an attractive, healthy stud for a husband.”

“Yay! Lucky me,” she replied, sarcasm at maximum. She watched him find even this amusing. Everything about her seemed to delight him, including her deliberate provocations. His standards were famously high, a fact she’d counted on when crafting her approach, yet somehow she’d cleared them with room to spare.

⁂

After demolishing four pancakes liberally slathered in peanut butter, a healthy serving of eggs, and a bowl of fruit salad, Michael rose from the table.

“I have to run now, I’m almost late,” he called as Rebecca returned to the kitchen to finish the egusi soup.

She completed her preparations methodically, listening to his movements in the living room. The space perfectly showcased Michael’s taste, exquisite and expensive. Everything was white, gold, or some elegant combination, giving their home the air of modern royalty that he preferred.

“Wait! Don’t forget your lunch.” Rebecca hurried into the living room carrying the bag containing his lunch flask just as Michael got to the door.

He grabbed it quickly, already pulling the door open.

“Aren’t you forgetting something?” she asked.

Michael smiled as if suddenly remembering, and planted a kiss on her lips.

Rebecca giggled. “Sweet. But I meant this.” She produced a checkered blue tie from behind her back. This daily ritual had become second nature—his protest, her insistence, his eventual surrender.

“Oh come on! Not today too.” Michael protested, raising his free hand. “I hate ties, they feel like…”

“Chains,” she finished for him. They both laughed as she secured the tie around his neck, her fingers working with practiced precision.

“I more than like you,” he said, caressing her face before kissing her lips.

“I more than like you too,” she replied, the words catching in her throat. The response had become automatic, reflexive. Yet sometimes, in moments like this, she wondered whether she was still acting.

As Michael departed, Rebecca stood in the doorway, watching him walk to his car. The warm July breeze played with her nightgown as a single, unwelcome thought surfaced: How was it possible that she genuinely meant those words? To him, of all people?

Chapter Three

Brawl

In Amaka’s four years as Michael’s secretary at Trident Independent Adjusters, she’d held more unofficial roles than her generous salary could cover: Queen Apologist, Minister of Compassionate Affairs, Royal Stormcatcher, to name a few. When a storm approached Michael, even one he’d invariably created himself, she’d anticipate its source and trajectory, then defuse it with apologetic gifts bought with his money. Most times she succeeded. But that day, the storm burst through her defenses with a fury that needed no interpretation.

Kola’s large frame burst into Amaka’s office and erupted into Michael’s less than two seconds later, his voice blazing with anger. “You fucking animal, you worthless piece of shit.”

Amaka hurried after him, positioning herself carefully at the corner of Michael’s office. She shot warning glances at Michael, loaded with messages she wished she could transmit directly to his brain: See? I warned you. Please don’t make this worse, you stupid idiot.

If only one true thing could be said about Michael, it was that nothing fazed him. Even if Satan himself emerged from the fiery pits of hell, Michael would remain seated in his customized office chair, his throne, as cool and dry as the harmattan breeze in January. This unflappable demeanor only fueled Kola’s rage.

“How can you just sit there after what you’ve done? How can you even be in this building?” the large man bellowed.

“I’m not sure what’s going on with you, but last I checked, I work here,” Michael replied, diverting his eyes from his iMac—the company-issued ProBook wasn’t good enough for him—to the seething man before him.

From her position by the door, Amaka watched Kola’s fury burn white-hot. His face swelled as it always did when truly enraged, looking seconds away from emitting actual steam.

“I don’t know if you’ve noticed, but you’re hyperventilating. Calm down before you give yourself a heart attack,” Michael said. Amaka caught the hint of genuine concern beneath his condescension, a nuance Kola would surely miss.

Whether from the advice or having reached his anger’s peak, Kola’s expression gradually shifted toward something resembling control.

“How dare you? You had no right to speak to my clients.”

“I don’t get paid to explain your incompetence.”

“By taking advantage of what I told you in confidence? How could you sink that low?”

“I’m all the way up here,” Michael replied, spreading his arms. “It’s all about perspective. We did not swear an oath, nor are we in a relationship.”

Amaka winced at his callousness. This was Michael at his worst; coldly logical, utterly remorseless.

A heavy silence filled the office, the kind that follows statements so devastating they require extra seconds to process.

Amaka bit her blood-red artificial fingernail, a nervous habit she couldn’t shake. She clung to a thin hope that Michael’s callousness wouldn’t trigger another eruption from Kola.

Amaka watched the realization wash over Kola’s face; the painful understanding that someone he’d considered a friend saw him as nothing more than competition. His expression shifted from rage to something more vulnerable.

“Is that what you have to say, Michael?” he finally asked, his voice quieter, fury giving way to hurt.

Amaka noticed Michael studying Kola’s face with unusual attention. For a fleeting moment, something resembling guilt crossed his features before vanishing. He glanced at his Apple Watch, checking the time until his shopping date with Rebecca, no doubt.

“We may work in the same company,” Michael said, standing up, “but that doesn’t make us a team. If there’s competition, I compete. I offered your clients a better deal, fixed their problem, and secured the company more leverage. I’m not going to apologize for that.” He shouldered his lunch bag and moved toward the door.

“Bastard!” Kola spat.

Amaka saw it coming a second too late. In her years with Michael, she’d learned his triggers, and “bastard” was near the top of the list.

Before she could intervene, Michael transformed before her eyes, his composed exterior giving way to primal rage. He lunged at Kola with frightening speed, his fist connecting with the larger man’s jaw in a sickening crack.

Kola stumbled back against Michael’s desk but recovered quickly. With a roar, he shoved Michael across the room with enough force to send him staggering. Pain and fury flashed across Kola’s face as he charged forward, attempting to tackle Michael to the ground.

But Michael wasn’t an easy target. Amaka had seen him box at the company gym. She knew the lean muscle beneath his tailored suits wasn’t just for show. He sidestepped Kola’s rush with practiced precision, tripping the larger man and pouncing before he could recover. Michael straddled him, landing two vicious punches.

Amaka grabbed the nearest object and hurled it against the far wall. The glass paperweight shattered spectacularly, sending shards dancing in the afternoon sun.

“You FOOLS!” Amaka bellowed, baseball bat gripped in white-knuckled hands. “So help me God, I will smash the head of the next person that moves.”

The men froze.

Amaka knew from experience: sometimes you had to be crazier than the crazy people to bring them back to their senses.

⁂

Minutes later, with Kola gone, Amaka swept up broken glass while Michael watched from his chair. She paused occasionally to glare at him with enough heat to melt steel.

“I didn’t know you kept a bat in the office,” he said, attempting to lighten the atmosphere. “Nice weapon.”

Her eyes remained unrelenting in their condemnation.

“What? It was a confrontation!”

“A confrontation that didn’t need to happen,” she replied coolly. “What exactly did you gain from creating discord? From hurting another person? You could have helped him fix the deal rather than stealing it.” Her eyes searched Michael’s for any flicker of remorse. As always, they found none.

He looked away. “It’s a man-eat-man world out there. You do what you have to do. If not, someone else will do it, and then you blame fate.”

Amaka’s disappointment was so profound it seemed to physically affect him. He shifted uncomfortably under her gaze.

“Sorry you had to witness that,” he said, an obvious attempt to escape her judgment. “I’m going out to get a gift for the Queen with Rebecca. What should I get you?”

The anger coursing through her overwhelmed her usual filter. Words meant to remain unspoken tumbled from her lips: “I don’t see what she sees in you.”

Chapter Four

Two Cases of Mistaken Identity

The bright sunlight filtered through the glass walls of the gift shop, illuminating the bustling city streets several floors below. Michael’s red GT Camaro stood out among the vehicles parked along the sidewalk.

“You look like you’re in a hurry,” Michael observed, noting Rebecca’s restless movements as they browsed the home decor aisle.

“Aren’t we all?” She slipped on her sunglasses with dramatic flair. “Life is short. Haven’t you realized that yet, boy?”

Michael couldn’t help but laugh. She had this way of cutting through his darker moods. Hard to believe that just forty-five minutes ago, he’d been consumed with rage during the confrontation with Kola.

“I’ve thought about that,” he said, once his amusement subsided. “What if science found a way to extend our lives by three or four hundred years, and it came in an injection? Would you…”

“No, thank you,” she interrupted. “This is plenty long enough for me.”

“That was quick,” Michael remarked, pausing to examine an ornate flower vase. “So much for ‘till death do us part.’”

Rebecca laughed freely. “What are you talking about?”

“Well, what if I decided to stay? Would you go without me?”

“That’s a completely different question.” She tapped her chin thoughtfully. “I’d need to conduct a cost analysis, plus draft a happiness-time-spent graph. And don’t get me started on how absolutely fucked up the world will be in a hundred years, let alone four hundred.”

Michael’s quiet anxiety gave way to genuine laughter as she continued her elaborate analysis.

“All things considered, there’s a lot of thinking to be done. I’ll give you a definite answer when we’re a year into this marriage,” she concluded, crossing to inspect a display of kitchen decor that had caught her interest.

“Fair enough. I’ll have to wait for your decision before making mine,” he replied, selecting a cream-colored Happy Birthday to My World card from a display stand. “I happen to love your cooking a little too much to decide hastily.”

“Just my cooking?” She glanced back at him, eyebrow raised.

“Hmm... what else did you think?” He pretended to consider. “Maybe the sex too... definitely the sex. Oh, and your foolishness, of course. Beyond that, can’t say there’s much more.”

“Definitely the sex,” she agreed with a playful grin that made his pulse quicken.

⁂

The couple stood at the counter, waiting for their change. Michael had selected a card, a bouquet of striking red flowers, and a transparent vase shaped like a mountain, which, with the right arrangement, would create the illusion of a miniature ice mountain sprouting what he’d described to Rebecca as “Flava: flowery lava.” She’d rolled her eyes at the name but approved of the gift.

Rebecca’s current frustration stemmed from the temporarily malfunctioning cash register. The cashier had gone to find assistance. She returned moments later with Kalu, the manager. His near-symmetrical face bore the evidence of an ongoing battle with acne since puberty. Despite the rebellious state of his skin, he maintained a clean, professional appearance. He bent over the register, fingers working to resolve the issue.

“What happened to that bride?” Rebecca asked suddenly, turning to face Michael.

“Huh?”

“The record holder. The one who killed her husband at the reception. Did she get away with it?”

“Sorry for the delay,” Kalu interrupted, offering Michael the POS machine. “It’s working now.”

“Oh, she didn’t wait around to find out,” Michael replied as he entered his pin. Upon completing the payment, they left the shop.

⁂

“She escaped?” Rebecca asked as they stepped from the cool, artificial air of the shopping center into the scorching heat outside.

“No, silly. She killed herself. Best thing she could have done, given the circumstances.”

“Excuse me,” a voice called from behind them.

They turned to find Kalu approaching, a branded shopping bag in his left hand. He took long strides to catch up with them.

“You forgot this–” Kalu’s voice choked off, his eyes wide with shock as they locked on Rebecca. “Sarah!”

Rebecca glanced over her shoulder, confusion creasing her face.

“I never thought I’d see you again!” he rushed on, his grin collapsing into a stunned blink at her blank stare.

“Thank you for bringing this,” Rebecca said, taking the bag from his hand. “But I’m afraid I’m not who you think I am.”

“Sarah Okolo?” Kalu persisted, his excitement deflating.

“Rebecca. Rebecca Azodo,” she corrected, gesturing toward Michael who stood a few feet behind her. “And this is my husband.”

“I’m so sorry,” Kalu stammered, embarrassed. “You look exactly like someone I used to know.”

“It’s alright,” she replied good-naturedly. “Some people believe in doppelgängers, right? I guess I have one.”

“Yeah,” he agreed with a nervous chuckle.

The conversation faded into the ambient noise of the city. Michael grew impatient, gently taking Rebecca by the arm and steering her toward the Camaro.

⁂

As they merged into traffic, Michael’s mind gnawed at the encounter. “That’s the second time you’ve been mistaken for someone else. You must look like a lot of people.”

“What can I say? I descend from a long line of warrior princesses,” Rebecca replied, watching the tall, brown government buildings slide past her window.

“What does that have to do with two cases of mistaken identity?”

“Not a thing.” She smiled sheepishly.

Michael’s fingers tightened on the steering wheel as he recalled the incident in Benin. A girl had approached them at the airport and called Rebecca by another name. He’d found it amusing then, but now curiosity nibbled at the edges of his mind.

“Seriously though, I find it curious. Don’t you?”

Rebecca stared out at the federal secretariat buildings. Massive concrete structures housing various ministries soon to disgorge thousands of workers as the day ended.

“What can I say? I’m an enigma,” she replied lightly.

“I thought you were a warrior princess?”

“Duh! A warrior princess can also be an enigma.”

“Fair point,” Michael conceded.

As they drove on, he couldn’t entirely dismiss the strange coincidence from his mind.

Chapter Five

Unwanted Life

Rebecca arrived home early that evening, confirming with relief that Michael wasn’t back yet. Perfect. It was that time of the month again, the sixth consecutive month, and she needed privacy for what came next.

She stood in the white-tiled bathroom of their five-bedroom luxury home, staring at the plastic pregnancy test stick in her trembling hand. Her heart hammered against her ribs, while her lungs seemed to have forgotten how to function. The house maintained an eerie silence around her, as if the walls themselves were holding their breath, waiting for the verdict: life or death.

Beep!

Her breath caught, the silence stretching as she stared at the blank window, willing it to stay empty. Then they appeared—two pink lines, sharp as a verdict. Her heart sank to her feet. Life.

Rebecca stared at the test, her fingers suddenly numb. The irony wasn’t lost on her: most women prayed for this result, while she had been praying against it for months. Everything had just become infinitely more complicated.

Chapter Six

The Queen’s Party

Tuxedos mingled with elegant gowns, formal shirts paired with tailored pantsuits, and colorful caftans added vibrant touches throughout the room. The Queen’s birthday celebration had drawn Abuja’s upper class—those fortunate enough to be in Lydia Azodo’s social circle. Champagne glasses clinked as the golden liquid flowed freely, lubricating conversations that filled the air with laughter and gossip. In the far corner of the parlor-turned-ballroom, a live band played tunes that seemed to make even the chandeliers shimmer.

Michael surveyed the scene with quiet approval. His mother had pulled out all the stops. For someone who preferred quiet, intimate celebrations, she had surpassed herself. The band’s high-life rendition of Asa’s Fire on the Mountain faded as a gentle “Clink! Clink!” sounded through the speakers. The room quieted, attention shifting toward the small stage. Michael stepped to the microphone, resplendent in a gray three-piece suit. The Queen deserved nothing less than his best.

“Welcome, ladies and gentlemen,” he began, surveying the crowd with practiced ease. “I want to thank you all for coming. Though I suspect some of you are here merely to avoid the consequences of not coming.”

The room erupted in knowing laughter.

“We gather to celebrate a truly phenomenal woman. My wife and I refer to her as the Queen.” He paused, gesturing toward Rebecca, who stood near the back in a stunning red gown. “Actually, the nickname was my wife’s idea.”

Rebecca forced a polite smile for the curious onlookers. Michael caught her stony glance and the subtle warning in her eyes: You’ll pay for that later.

“And it’s not far from the truth,” Michael continued, his voice softening. “When I was young, the night my father left us after a terrible fight, my mother was devastated. I was a mess too. Something else had happened to me that day.”

He paused, momentarily lost in memory. “She took my hand in hers and said, ‘Life goes on. We’ll go to bed in tears tonight, but tomorrow we’ll wake up and try to smile. And if we cry again tomorrow, we’ll try again the next day, and the day after that, until we finally do smile. Because life doesn’t stop for anyone, and neither should we.’”

After a moment’s reflection, he raised his glass. “Please join me in toasting a remarkable African woman, an accomplished psychologist, and the strongest person I know. To Lydia Azodo, my loving mother, who is indeed more than a queen.”

Laughter rippled through the crowd. As people began to look around, Michael spotted Lydia emerging from a cluster of guests at the far end of the room. She was dressed in a simple white gown that complemented her graceful bearing, her makeup subtle but flawless. Age had only enhanced her natural elegance; every movement radiated the poise of a woman comfortable in her own skin.

Michael stepped down from the stage with a brief bow and made his way toward his mother. As he walked, Rebecca moved from her position at the back to join him, and he took her hand as they approached Lydia together.

“If I’m the queen, that must make you both the princess and the frog,” Lydia said as they reached her.

“After my stunning toast?” Michael feigned hurt, which prompted laughter from all three.

“What’s wrong, dear?” Lydia asked, reaching out to touch Rebecca’s arm.

“Nothing at all,” Rebecca replied, visibly stretching her smile wider.

Rebecca always seemed to be performing, wearing a mask that didn’t quite fit. “Sure,” Lydia said, waving them off. “You two should try the cake. It’s red velvet, very nice.”

As his mother stepped away to fulfill her hostess obligations, Michael settled into a nearby chair. He reached for an unopened bottle of Hennessy, suddenly feeling the need for something stronger than champagne.

“I know it’s my party,” she said, settling beside him, “but perhaps you should pace yourself.”

“I’m fine,” he replied, his steady voice belying the amount he’d consumed. “What were we discussing?”

Michael’s expression darkened. “The dreams have returned. I’ve run out of the pills.”

Lydia’s expression tightened at his tone. “Michael.”

“Sorry,” he muttered, properly chastised.

They sat in silence for a moment, the party’s festive atmosphere swirling around them in stark contrast to their somber conversation.

“I feel like these dreams must mean something,” Michael finally said, his voice lower.

“I’ve already told you, they don’t mean any–” Lydia broke off mid-sentence, her attention suddenly captured by something across the room.

Michael followed her gaze to see a man entering the party. The sight of him made his heart stutter in his chest.

“Excuse me,” Lydia said, rising quickly.

The transformation was quick. The poised hostess everyone had seen earlier was replaced by a woman suddenly decades younger, her face luminous with unexpected joy.

As he made his way across the room, Michael caught sight of Amaka and Rebecca by the dessert table. Amaka had frozen mid-sentence, her attention fixed on the same man. He watched as she frantically pointed, saying something to Rebecca before abandoning her plate of cake and rushing toward them.

Even from this distance, Michael could read Amaka’s expression perfectly: This could get ugly.

“You look as beautiful as ever,” Martin said, a sheepish smile softening his normally stern features as he took Lydia into his arms.

Michael watched the exchange from several feet away, struck by how quickly his mother’s professional demeanor melted into something warmer, more vulnerable. The way they fit together spoke of a shared past that excluded him entirely. His grip tightened on his glass of Hennessy.

“And you, as handsome as I remember,” Lydia replied.

Michael edged closer, unsettled by his mother’s transformation. Gone was the formidable psychologist. In her place stood a woman he barely recognized, gazing up at Martin with unmistakable affection.

“I was in town for a meeting, and I thought to drop by. Happy birthday,” Martin said, presenting her with a small, elegantly wrapped box.

“Thank you, Martin. I deeply appreciate it.” She accepted the gift and leaned forward to plant a kiss on his cheek, a gesture of intimacy that made Michael’s stomach twist.

The alcohol coursed warmly through Michael’s veins, softening the sharp edges of resentment that typically accompanied thoughts of Martin and transforming his usual caution into reckless courage. After all, this was his mother’s birthday. Perhaps they could put aside old wounds for one night. Without fully considering the consequences, Michael stepped toward the pair, arms opening. “Hello Dad.”

From the corner of his eye, Michael glimpsed Amaka rushing toward them, her expression alarmed, but too late to prevent what was already unfolding.

“I am not your father,” Martin stated, his voice loud enough to carry to nearby guests. He stepped back, physically rejecting Michael’s attempt at an embrace.

The conversation around them faltered. Heads turned. What should have been a private moment of rejection became a public spectacle, with Michael center stage.

Something snapped inside Michael. Whether it was the public humiliation burning his cheeks, the familiar sting of paternal rejection, or the Hennessy amplifying both—he couldn’t say. What he knew was that fifteen years of contained resentment suddenly demanded release.

His fingers loosened, the crystal tumbler slipping from his grip. Before the glass shattered against the marble floor, his fist was already connecting with Martin’s face in a sickening crack.

“Fuck you!” The words exploded from him.

“Michael!” Lydia’s voice sliced through his rage.

Martin staggered backward, hand pressed to his bleeding lip. Michael’s satisfaction had only lasted a second when his mother’s palm connected with his left cheek.

The slap echoed in the sudden silence. Michael stood frozen, the burn on his cheek secondary to the weight of dozens of stares. He’d orchestrated this entire evening to honor his mother, positioned himself as the devoted son before all these people, and now stood exposed and humiliated. The perfect image he’d crafted of himself and his family lay shattered like the glass at his feet.

Chapter Seven

The People We Love

Lydia guided Martin to the second-floor guest bathroom, as far from prying eyes as possible. Her sixtieth birthday celebration continued below, the band’s stripped-down rendition of Bob Marley’s No Woman No Cry filtering up through the floor.

“You antagonized him,” she said, dampening a plush hand towel to tend to his face. The punch had split his lip, leaving a bright smear of blood at the corner of his mouth. The silver-framed mirror above the marble sink reflected them both: the elegant psychologist and her distinguished ex-husband, still bound by complicated history.

“Antagonized him? He punched me,” Martin replied, incredulous. Despite approaching sixty himself, his face retained the boyish indignation that had once charmed her.

“He wanted a hug from his father, but you—”

“I made it clear fifteen years ago, I am not his father.”

“Jesus! Why are some of you men so stubborn?” Lydia exploded, years of frustration bubbling to the surface. “Why won’t you let go of the past? It was a bloody mistake!”

“A mistake that cost a life,” Martin replied, his businessman’s poise reasserting itself as he dabbed at his split lip. “You still defend him after all these years?”

“You still blame him after all these years?” she shot back, rolling her eyes. “He is my son, what am I supposed to do, allow the world crucify him while I stand and watch?”

“You saw what just happened downstairs,” Martin continued, gesturing at his injured face. “How quick to violence he was. Can’t you see? He hasn’t changed.”

Lydia recognized the familiar pattern of their argument, the same dance they’d performed countless times over fifteen years. This was the moment where Martin always expected her to choose rational judgment over maternal instinct, to side with the wronged husband rather than the troubled son. Each time, he presented his case like a closing argument, all evidence and logical progression.

And each time, he failed to understand that a mother’s love operated by different rules. No amount of proof, logic, or reason could overcome it. Lydia knew this about herself; knew her blindness where Michael was concerned was both her greatest strength and most profound weakness.

Rather than meet Martin’s searching gaze, she busied herself again with the first aid kit, cleaning his injuries with a gentleness that belied her inner turmoil.

She felt Martin’s frustration building before he finally broke. “Does he even know what he’s done?”

The question hung between them. Lydia’s inability to answer told him everything.

“My God! Maybe I should tell—”

“You would not dare!” The words burst from her with such vehemence that Martin physically recoiled. In that moment, the polished psychologist vanished, replaced by something primal and fierce.

Seeing his startled expression, Lydia recognized she’d revealed too much. She modulated her voice, though the intensity remained. “You may not love him anymore, but he can never know what he did. It would destroy him.” She swallowed hard, then added the plea she hated herself for making, “If you ever truly loved me, you’ll keep that past buried where it belongs.”

Martin’s expression softened as he looked at her. For a moment, years fell away, and Lydia could see traces of the man she’d fallen for at that airport; the one whose nervousness had charmed her, whose ambition had impressed her, whose love had once seemed unshakeable.

“I do still love you,” he said simply.

The words sent a jolt through her. After six years without seeing him, and fifteen years since their marriage ended, those five words still held power. Lydia felt a sudden hope flare in her chest. Dangerous, foolish hope. She looked away quickly, not wanting him to see it.

“Is it really so hard for you to forgive him?” she asked softly.

“I could never forgive him for coming between us.”

The hope extinguished as quickly as it had appeared. “No, Martin. Your inability to forgive him for a mistake is what came between us,” she replied, her voice cooling as she handed him the towel and moved toward the door. With each step away from him, Lydia felt the familiar closure of their dance. Another argument ending with neither yielding.

“It’s the things and people we love that destroy us,” Martin said from behind her, his voice carrying that familiar note of certainty. The successful businessman dispensing wisdom.

Lydia paused at the doorway, her back still to him. The irony struck her: their love was simultaneously too strong to die completely and too weak to overcome this obstacle. Those romantic movies had it all wrong; love wasn’t always enough.

“I guess some things don’t change,” she said quietly, not trusting herself to look back.

“I guess they don’t,” Martin replied, his tone measured and controlled.

She walked out, grateful he couldn’t see the tears sliding down her cheeks.

Chapter Eight

Let’s Have A Baby

The couple were back home, getting ready for bed. Michael settled onto his side of the bed, feeling somewhat refreshed from his shower, though the alcohol wasn’t going to release him without exacting its toll of a mild headache. The silk sheets felt impossibly smooth against his skin as he stretched out, trying to dispel the lingering tension from the evening’s disaster. A thought crossed his mind, one he’d never seriously considered before tonight.

Rebecca, meanwhile, was weathering her own private storm. The bathroom’s fluorescent brightness stood in stark contrast to the darkness building inside her. Her red silk nightgown caught the light as she stared at her reflection, wondering how she could have been so careless. The third pregnancy test she had taken today lay in her trembling hand. The more she hoped that the new result would negate the previous two, the more that hope was crushed by the weight of two unmistakable pink lines.

The only thing keeping her composed was knowing she wouldn’t have to discuss it tonight. Michael’s confrontation with his father had ensured the evening would revolve around him. Perhaps it wouldn’t be as catastrophic as she feared. Perhaps she shouldn’t feel this terrified. But she knew her fear went beyond normal anxiety; it was horror.

So absorbed in her emotional turbulence, Rebecca didn’t register Michael speaking to her from the bedroom.

“Rebecca?” he called again, louder this time.

“Yes!” Startled back to reality, she quickly returned the test to its packaging and tucked it into the cabinet where she kept her personal items, the cabinet Michael never bothered to open. His disinterest in anything that didn’t directly concern him had proven useful; she’d learned that hiding things in plain sight was often the most effective strategy. He simply never looked.

“Let’s have a baby. I think I’d like to have a son.”

“What?!” Rebecca half-shouted, unable to mask her shock.

The sharp response jolted Michael from his contemplative mood. Had he said something wrong? Had he angered her? Why was she so hostile? He thought women generally welcomed such suggestions. Wasn’t this the sort of thing they wanted to hear? He lay confused in silence.

Rebecca instantly recognized her mistake. She took several deep breaths, checked her reflection one final time, and emerged from the bathroom with her composure restored, just as Michael was saying in a softer tone, “I think I—”

“I heard you,” Rebecca interrupted, “we’ve never had this conversation before, why now?” she asked in a voice with enough warmth to melt Antarctica’s ice sheets.

Encouraged by her tone, Michael considered the question. To his surprise, the answer came without effort, as though he’d been carrying it unknowingly.

“I’d like to be a better father than I had.”

Despite Rebecca’s internal chaos, something about the raw honesty in his voice reached her. This was Michael at his most genuine, a rare glimpse beneath the armor. No matter her feelings about him or herself, she remained attuned to authentic emotion. His yearning touched something in her that transcended their complicated relationship. The pain behind his motivation, the honesty of his desire—she felt these things as keenly as if they were her own. This empathic connection was what had led to her original mistake; she could never help responding to genuine vulnerability.

She moved closer, drawing his head against her chest in a gesture that was both maternal and intimate. Without words, she communicated understanding.

Michael marveled at her ability to comfort him without speaking. The question of having children suddenly seemed secondary to this moment of connection. He sensed she wasn’t ready but felt no urgency to press the issue. Still, there was no harm in practicing. Without raising his face, he began trailing kisses from one silk-covered breast to the other, gradually working his way down her body, pausing to lavish attention on each curve and hollow.

Rebecca offered no resistance. Whatever her feelings about the pregnancy, about Michael, about their tangled history, physical connection remained uncomplicated. And if she were being honest with herself, he knew exactly how to make her body respond.

Chapter Nine

An Unexpected Fall

“I didn’t hear you drive in. I thought it was a burglar,” Michael said groggily as he entered the kitchen in his pajamas to find Rebecca in her element. She was dressed in her white nurse uniform with blue-streaked hair neatly contained. Outside the window, dawn was just beginning to assert itself, the night reluctantly yielding to the new day.

Rebecca gave him a puzzled look. “A burglar that came to steal pots and plates?”

“A hungry burglar.”

“You sha don’t have sense,” she said, her staccato laugh bouncing off the cream tiles.

Maybe it was morning desire, or perhaps it was her laugh, but Michael closed the distance between them and slipped his arms around her waist, kissing her neck as she stirred the steaming oatmeal.

“Do you want burnt oatmeal?” she protested weakly.

“I want hot Rebecca,” he murmured against her skin, then traced his tongue along the sensitive spot behind her right ear. He felt her muscles relax as he ran his hands over her curves, her body responding to his touch despite her half-hearted objection.

Normally, she would yield to him willingly. He prided himself on knowing exactly how to touch her, how to bring her to the edge and beyond with practiced precision. His skill at unraveling her was matched only by his patience in doing so. Under different circumstances, she would have abandoned the oatmeal without a second thought.

But something in her posture told him this morning was different. Though her body responded, her mind seemed elsewhere. Michael sensed resistance, not to him specifically, but to the delay he represented.

“As much as I would love to finish this, I need to hurry with this and go back,” Rebecca said, pulling out of his embrace. He watched, disappointed, as she poured the steaming oatmeal into her breakfast flask.

“Do you always have to go back?” Michael asked, making no effort to hide his displeasure.

Rebecca nodded silently, brushing past him to retrieve eggs from the fridge.

“The same old man?”

Another nod. “He has no one else to care for him.”

“So it has to be you?” Michael asked, his lips forming a petulant pout.

She laughed at his expression. “When you sulk like that, you really look like a frog.”

Just then, the doorbell chimed. Michael carried his sulk out of what he now mentally labeled as “Rebecca’s kitchen,” past the dining area, and toward the front door.

He opened it to find Amaka on the doorstep, dressed in a tailored grey suit and jacket, her professional appearance at odds with the mischievous glint in her eyes.

“Frog, is the Princess ready?” she asked, pushing past him and striding into the dimly lit parlor.

Rebecca’s laugh echoed from the kitchen, confirming she’d heard the exchange.

“Very funny, Basketmouth and Seyi Law,” Michael said, attempting to sound annoyed despite being mildly amused. “It’s getting old though.”

“Not to me,” Amaka replied flippantly.

“Nor me, Prince Naveen,” Rebecca chimed in, poking her head from the kitchen doorway.

Michael heaved a deep sigh and headed toward the black marble staircase beside the massive leather couch.

“Are you ready?” Amaka asked.

“Yeah,” Rebecca replied, walking into the living room with her bag.

“Don’t forget your one o’clock with the Queen,” Amaka called out to Michael, who paused at the foot of the staircase. She helped herself to a slice of peanut-buttered bread from his breakfast plate.

“Is that today?”

“Yup, one month since her birthday,” Amaka confirmed, casually walking out the door with another slice of his bread.

Forty minutes later, Michael finished what remained of his breakfast—the bread Amaka had left behind, plus a healthy serving of milk-soaked oatmeal and scrambled eggs. He headed for the door dressed in his customary black suit, crisp white shirt, gleaming Italian shoes, and leather office bag slung across his body. As he reached for the doorknob, a blue post-it note caught his eye. In Rebecca’s distinctive red handwriting:

Oh, Pompous Prince,

YOU SHALL NOT PASS!!!

Hence, go back and retrieve thy substance of midday sustenance from the Royal Majesty’s glorious table of food and bodily banquet.

P.S. Thine object of neckly splendour lay below the lonely arrow yonder.

P.P.S. Oshey Men In Black! Abeg get me Will Smith’s autograph.

Michael felt laughter bubble up inside him. He dutifully retrieved the tie from beneath the note and backtracked to the kitchen for the black lunch bag Rebecca had been preparing for him every day since they got married six months ago. Returning to the door, he scrawled his response beneath her message:

I HATE ties, but anything for my Kitchen Ninja.

⁂

The restaurant buzzed with lunchtime chatter, silverware clinking against ceramic plates as servers meandered between tables. Brown wallpaper adorned with oversized black flower patterns covered the walls. Across from Michael, his mother stared with undisguised fascination at the black lunch bag he’d placed on the table.

“Not to judge,” Lydia said, though her tone suggested otherwise, “but I still can’t wrap my head around the fact that you bring your own lunch to the restaurant.”

“I’m not mad,” Michael replied with a grin. “There’s a perfectly good dining room at the office. This was the only free time I’d have to eat today.” He took his ATM card from the slender waitress, dismissing her with his characteristic wave.

His mother’s blank expression told him she wasn’t buying it; after all, the Michael of eight months ago would have rather starved than commit the social faux pas of bringing homemade food to a restaurant. She had always been difficult to deceive. Years of experience had taught him that with her, honesty was the only viable strategy. Her accusations rarely came directly. More often than not, they arrived as casual observations preceded by one of her numerous looks. Michael had catalogued at least seven distinct accusatory gazes so far and harbored ambitions of mastering his responses to all of them within the next few years.

“You can’t blame me,” he continued, raising his hands in mock surrender. “She insists on preparing the food herself. She even comes home at 5 AM when she’s on night duty.”

He paused, then admitted, “And if we’re being entirely honest, her cooking is better than what they serve here. So no complaints from me.”

Her slow, deliberate nods suggested she’d accepted his explanation, though Michael couldn’t resist adding as they stood to leave, “The real embarrassment would be going back to these inferior meals at outlandish prices.”

“How about the dreams?” Lydia asked as they waited beneath the plaza’s glass dome entrance, her driver bringing the car around.

“They’re gone. The medication works,” Michael said, then hesitated. “But…”

“But what?”

“Don’t worry, it’s nothing,” he said, attempting reassurance. But there it was—accusatory gaze number seven, the one that combined concern with penetrating skepticism. His resistance crumbled instantly.

“I know you said they mean nothing,” he admitted, “but they feel too real, like old memories I never knew I had.”

A black Mercedes glided to a stop before them. Lydia stepped toward the car, then turned back to face him.

“Speaking as both your mother and your lifetime psychiatrist, I assure you they mean nothing. Trust me.”

How could he doubt her? She was the expert, the one person who had stood by him through everything. He leaned in for a hug and kissed her cheek.

“Take care of yourself,” she said, squeezing his hand before settling into the backseat.

Michael watched the car pull away, her certainty lingering like a protective shield. The tension drained from his shoulders.

She was right. She was always right. As long as the medication kept the dreams at bay, there was no reason to worry about their meaning. A non-issue. Reassured, he strutted across the sun-drenched parking lot toward his car, already planning the rest of his day.

“Oh, shit!” He patted his pockets, realizing he’d left his keys on the restaurant table. With a frustrated sigh, he turned back toward the glass-fronted plaza.

That’s when everything changed.

Color began draining from the world, one hue at a time—first green, then blue, then yellow—until all that remained was lifeless gray. A thunderous pounding filled his chest, making it impossible to focus. His heart felt like it was being squeezed in a vise, palpitating at a dangerous rhythm. His lungs refused to draw air, each attempt more painful than the last.

The sounds around him warped and faded, replaced by the roar of his own labored breathing. One step forward became an unexpected fall, a terrifying descent into darkness. The last thing Michael registered was the hard impact as his body struck the parking lot pavement.

Chapter Ten

A Promise Made

Michael woke up hours later, his mind still haunted by fragments of his recurring nightmare. The falling boy. The blood-stained hands. The crushing weight of guilt and fear. He’d just complained to his mother about these dreams, and now they seemed more visceral than ever; vivid yet incomprehensible, like memories that belonged to someone else.

“You’re still alive, just in case you were wondering,” a young chatty voice said in the dark.

That’s when Michael realized he had no idea where he was. His mind might have escaped the dark void of unconsciousness, but his vision was clearly still catching up. His senses activated one by one, like an emergency system coming online. His skin registered a soft surface beneath him—a bed, most likely. His eyes gradually adjusted to distinguish vague outlines of a rectangular room. The sharp smell of antiseptic cut through his mental fog, narrowing the possibilities. He wondered if the cheerful voice was real or just another product of his disoriented mind.

As if reading his mind, the voice came again. “I was wondering when you’d wake up. It gets boring here on nights when there’s nobody to talk to, so I was happy when they brought you in.” A slight pause. “I’m sorry, I know that’s selfish.”

The pieces clicked into place in Michael’s mind. A hospital ward. He couldn’t make out much in the darkness. The lights were off, and he couldn’t tell if there were windows. His body felt leaden, too weak to sit up, though he managed to move his left arm, fingers finding the intravenous line taped to his right wrist.

With this first mystery solved, his mind turned to other questions. Who had found him? Was his mother the Good Samaritan? How—

“Are you sick... like me?” the voice interrupted.

...had he gotten here? What—

“Will you be my friend?”

...happened? Why was he—

“Are you always this quiet, or did your mum tell you not to speak to strangers too? I always tell my mum that if all the mommies in the world tell their children ‘don’t speak to strangers,’ how will strangers ever become friends?”

...he couldn’t string together a coherent…

“Is something wrong with your mouth?”

“Would you please shut up? I’m trying to think!” Michael snapped, his patience exhausted by the barrage of questions.

“I was just trying to be friends,” the voice replied, its bright chattiness giving way to palpable hurt.

Michael felt a twinge of... something. Not guilt; he’d mastered the art of dismissing that particular emotion long ago. More like mild irritation at the discomfort caused by the sudden silence. Still, true to form, he replied coolly, “I’m not interested, kid.”

Right then, a door to the ward opened with a soft squeak. A second later, fluorescent lights blazed to life, temporarily blinding Michael. He blinked rapidly as a pretty young nurse came into focus. She rolled an oxygen cylinder toward a bed three spaces to his right, where a young boy lay curled on his side.

“Cap, what’s wrong?” she asked, genuine concern in her voice.

The boy responded by turning further away, presenting his back to both the nurse and Michael.

“Excuse me,” Michael called out, more interested in finding out what was wrong with him.

“You’re awake,” the nurse said, her expression brightening. “How are you feeling?”

The Marvel reference wasn’t lost on Michael, though he had more pressing concerns than the nurse’s playful banter.

He’d only been hospitalized twice in his life—at least that he could remember—and the environment made him restless. Standing in the wide blue-and-white corridor, he felt surprisingly steady. Apart from a lingering headache, he seemed fine. The sooner he saw the doctor, the sooner he could leave. Faced with a choice between right and left, he followed the sound of voices.

A conversation drifted from the left, two female voices, one familiar. He followed the sound and turned the corner to find two nurses with their backs to him: one large and imposing, the other petite—Betty. He pressed against the wall, uncharacteristically interested in something that didn’t directly concern him.

“...he wouldn’t tell me what was wrong. I’m worried because that’s only the second time I’ve seen him sad in almost a year,” said Betty’s gentle voice.

“Chai! I dey feel for the boy sha. To sabi say him dey die as him small like dat. If na me, I no know wetin I for do o,” the Matron replied, her deep voice struggling to convey sympathy rather than pity.

Michael mentally processed this exchange: The annoyingly chatty boy he had shushed earlier was battling a terminal illness?

“Oga wetin you dey find?” The Matron’s hostile tone matched her suspicious expression.

“It’s okay, Matron,” Betty intervened, stepping between them with a placating smile. She turned to Michael. “The doctor is in surgery and will be out soon. I’ll let him know as soon as he’s finished.”

“You can use mine,” Betty offered, extracting a smartphone from her uniform pocket.

After three unanswered calls to Rebecca, Michael returned the phone with a curt nod and headed back toward the ward.

“So arrogant,” the Matron hissed behind him.

“Anyone who doesn’t say please after every request is arrogant to you,” Betty replied with an amused smile.

Michael pretended not to hear, though privately he acknowledged the accuracy of the assessment. He returned to the ward, his steps slowing as he approached his bed. For the first time since waking, he really looked at the boy three beds away.

Apart from the oxygen tank positioned beside his steel-framed bed, the kid appeared surprisingly healthy—no visible wasting, no obvious signs of terminal illness. He was dressed in a gray Captain America t-shirt and blue joggers, his skinny arms emerging from the short sleeves. Despite the late hour, he remained wide awake, completely absorbed in the third issue of Civil War. A black backpack with the red Avengers logo had spilled its contents onto the floor, revealing a collection of more Marvel comics. A Captain America figurine stood sentinel on the bedside cupboard.

The boy was dying, yet surrounded himself with symbols of heroism and strength. The irony wasn’t lost on Michael.

It wasn’t that Michael never felt guilt; he’d just become a grandmaster at suppressing it in all its forms. The process was automatic: his subconscious would identify the unwelcome emotion, quarantine it, and file it away where his conscious mind couldn’t access it. He experienced only the end result—a vague discomfort quickly replaced by justification or indifference.

But something about the conversation he’d overheard in the corridor had breached these defenses. Guilt infiltrated his consciousness, multiplying rapidly like a virus, impossible to ignore. It demanded action. In a desperate attempt to silence this internal discomfort, Michael did something entirely out of character.

“Hey kid, I’m sorry about before. My name is Michael.”

The boy gave no indication he’d heard, his eyes never leaving the colorful pages of his comic.

Under normal circumstances, Michael would have walked away, writing the kid off as not worth the effort. Instead, he tried again.

“What’s yours?”

The boy turned his back, physically rejecting the overture.

“Come on, I said I’m sorry. Let’s be friends.” Michael heard the desperation in his own voice and barely recognized it.

“No!” The response was sharp and immediate.

“Why not?”

“You are not a nice person.”

Michael smiled despite himself. More surprising was his own reluctance to accept defeat. He actually wanted this kid to like him. Drawing on a long-forgotten piece of advice from his mother about his first boarding school experience, he changed tactics: find common ground.

“I like Captain America too,” he offered, nodding toward the action figure. “Captain America would forgive anyone, even Bucky.”

“I am not Cap, and you are not Bucky,” the boy replied flatly, though Michael noticed he hadn’t turned away further.

Progress. Thank you, mum.

Michael deepened his voice, mimicking Ultron from Avengers: Age of Ultron. “Well, how could you be? You are not worthy.”

Michael noticed the boy’s posture shift slightly, a barely perceptible indication of interest. He pressed his advantage.

“But you could be.”

Silence stretched between them. Michael was about to admit defeat when the boy finally spoke.

“How?”

“Simple. You only have to forgive me.”

The boy half-turned, revealing his profile. “So if I forgive you, I’ll be worthy of being Cap?”

“Better,” Michael countered, leaning forward. “You will be Cap, and if you accept, I’ll be Bucky. Then maybe you’ll teach me to be a nice person, just like Cap taught Bucky.”

Another silence fell as the boy considered the proposal. Michael recognized the moment from countless business negotiations—when the agreement seemed close but still uncertain. Drawing on his professional experience, he knew exactly what was needed: sweeten the deal.

“Okay, I’ll sweeten the deal further. We’ll go see Civil War at the cinema on opening day.”

“Promise?” The boy turned fully toward Michael for the first time, his expression guarded but hopeful.

“On my iron arm,” Michael vowed, lifting his left arm to mimic Bucky Barnes’ metal prosthetic.

A beat passed between them, a silent evaluation.

“So what do you say?”

“Oruamen.”

“What?”

“My name is Oruamen.”

“Nice to meet you, Oruamen. I’m Michael.”

A tentative smile crossed the boy’s face, the first genuine emotion Michael had seen from him. Something shifted in that moment, a connection formed that seemed somehow significant though neither could say why.

Michael couldn’t have known it then, but this unlikely friendship with a dying boy would eventually save his life.

⁂

Betty roused Michael from sleep a few minutes later.

“The doctor will see you now.”

He climbed out of bed and followed the nurse toward the door, but paused before exiting.

“Give me a minute,” he said. “Can I use your pen?”

Betty reached into her breast pocket and handed him a red ballpoint. Michael returned to Oruamen’s bedside, where the boy had finally fallen asleep, comic book still clutched in his hand. Carefully, so as not to wake him, Michael placed his business card on the bedside cabinet.

Beneath his printed name and contact information, he added a single word in his precise handwriting:

Bucky

It was, perhaps, the first genuine promise he had made to anyone besides Rebecca in years.

Chapter Eleven

BOOM!

“There must be a mistake.” Michael stared at Dr. Abuba across the polished desk, struggling to process what he’d just heard. The office suddenly felt airless. He’d never encountered anything so utterly senseless, so completely impossible. It was definitely a mistake. No other explanation made sense. He would bet his life on it; and in a way, that’s exactly what was at stake.

Dr. Abuba maintained his composure, his expression a practiced blend of clinical detachment and appropriate concern. Michael recognized this face—it was the one professionals wore when delivering life-altering news. Not pitying, not emotional, just straightforward.

“I assure you, Mr. Michael, we made no mistakes,” the doctor said, folding his hands atop the manila folder containing the test results. “We carried out the test multiple times, and each time, the results were the same. I know this is hard to believe, and I understand how devastating it could be if we were wrong. I suggest we conduct another series of tests to be as close to a hundred percent certain as possible, but we’ll need a new sample.”

“I’m sure you’ll find that you’re wrong,” Michael said, his voice tight with controlled fury. He pushed up from the chair with enough force to send it scraping against the floor. Blood pounded in his ears as he stalked out of the office, slamming the door behind him with a force that reverberated through the corridor.

Michael returned home in the barren hours before dawn, slipping into bed beside his sleeping wife. He lay rigid, staring at the ceiling as a thunderstorm raged in his mind. Despite the two hours that had passed since leaving the hospital, he couldn’t focus on anything beyond the doctor’s words. The implications were too monstrous to contemplate.

His eyes drifted to Rebecca’s peaceful face, softened in sleep. Her dark hair peeked through the edges of her bonnet, her breathing deep and even. No, it was impossible. Rather than torment himself with such nonsense, he focused on the gentle curve of her cheek, the slight part of her lips. He wouldn’t trouble her with this absurdity. She didn’t deserve to have her name smeared by some incompetent doctor’s mistake. Because that’s all it was, a mistake. It had to be.

By midday, Michael had returned to Abuba’s office, his jaw set with determination. He’d come to witness the doctor prove himself wrong and apologize for besmirching perfection. His black lunch bag—the one Rebecca had prepared that morning with her usual care—sat on the desk between them like prosecution evidence in a murder trial.

Dr. Abuba had been reviewing the new test results for nearly a minute, his expression unreadable. Michael couldn’t bring himself to sit. His body vibrated with tension, forcing him to pace the small office. Despite his certainty, an infinitesimal doubt had crept in overnight; a doubt that now loomed large enough to make his heart race and his palms sweat as he awaited the verdict.

“What does it say?” Michael demanded, stopping abruptly in front of the desk, his fingers digging into the backrest of the chair he refused to occupy.

Dr. Abuba looked up, his professional mask slipping for just a moment to reveal something Michael had refused to see yesterday—genuine compassion.

“I’m sorry, Mr. Michael. Your wife is trying to kill you.”

Michael’s body suddenly felt too heavy for his legs. He sank into the chair, his defiance evaporating like morning mist.

“Like yesterday, we found traces of mercury in your lunch,” Dr. Abuba continued, his voice seeming to come from very far away. “The levels are small enough not to cause immediate problems, but sufficient to kill you gradually over time. Interestingly, your breakfast was free of toxins, suggesting the poisoning is deliberate and targeted, not environmental.”

Michael barely registered the words. A single sentence hammered in his head on endless repeat: Your wife is trying to kill you. His mind flooded with disjointed images and memories, each taking on horrifying new context:

The wedding. Had it all been a setup from the beginning? Their playful competition. Was it just a cover for something darker? Her insistence on preparing all his meals. Was it opportunity masked as devotion? Her mysterious visits to the “old man” each morning. An accomplice? A resource for poisons?

Everything he thought he knew about his marriage, about Rebecca, was suddenly cast in sinister shadow.

“I’m going to have to report this to the police.”

“No! No police,” Michael snapped, the threat of external intervention pulling him sharply from his mental spiral.

The doctor remained silent, removing his glasses to rub his tired eyes.

“Do you understand me, Doc?” Michael leaned forward, his shock temporarily replaced by fierce determination.

“It is my duty to—”

“Your duty is to treat people and make them better,” Michael cut in, his voice low and dangerous. “This doesn’t concern you. She is my wife. Are we clear?” He fixed the doctor with the same intimidating stare he’d used to close countless business deals.

Dr. Abuba held his gaze for a long moment before nodding reluctantly and replacing his spectacles.

“We’re still awaiting your final blood work from the lab to conduct a complete analysis,” Dr. Abuba continued. “There might be more we can learn about exposure duration and…”

Michael barely heard him. Despite the evidence, despite the repeated tests, a tiny sliver of denial lingered in his consciousness. There had to be another explanation. There had to be.

The torture of uncertainty was unbearable. He needed to know the truth beyond any shadow of doubt. No police, no accusations, no confrontations—not yet. First, he needed irrefutable proof. He needed to catch his Kitchen Ninja in the act.

With grim determination settling over him like armor, Michael rose from his chair. He would find the truth himself, whatever it might cost him.
